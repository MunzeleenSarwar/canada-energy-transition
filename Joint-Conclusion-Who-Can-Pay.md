# Joint conclusion: who can pay for the transition

## The question

Part A showed that Canada's transition is happening in power and in
capital spending, not in what the country produces: the fossil share of
primary energy production rose to 88.5% in 2024, while utilities capital
spending is set to pass oil and gas in 2025. The companies that must fund
the transition earn their cash from the fuels the transition is meant to
replace. Part B tested whether their balance sheets can carry that
spending. This note joins the two parts: sustainability score against
credit strength, for the same 12 companies, in the same year, 2024.

## The answer in one paragraph

Five companies can pay comfortably: Barrick, Imperial, Agnico, Suncor
and Tourmaline combine a solid scorecard with leverage under one times
EBITDA and coverage above 11 times. Three more can pay: CNQ, Cenovus
and Cameco have sound balance sheets and lower scores, and the two
energy names at the bottom of the scorecard score low because Bill C-59
stripped their 2024 reports, not because they are weak. Two score well
but cannot pay comfortably: Nutrien and Methanex both scored 15 out of
20 and are the two most leveraged names in the set, at 3.04 and 4.05
times EBITDA. The last two are special cases: Teck is joint top of the
scorecard and holds $7,587 million of cash from the coal sale, but its
2024 free cash flow was only $155 million, so its ability to fund
transition investment depends on copper earnings, not on the cash pile
alone; West Fraser is net cash, so its low Springate score is a cyclical
earnings trough, not distress. The scorecard and the credit models
measure different things, and the companies that score highest are not
the ones with the strongest balance sheets.

bill of only $41 million in 2024, confirmed by interest paid of $42
million.

## The four groups

### Can pay comfortably: Barrick, Imperial, Agnico, Suncor, Tourmaline

These five pair a score of 14 or better with leverage under one times
EBITDA and coverage above 11 times. Agnico is the strongest balance
sheet in the set: 0.29 times leverage, operating cash flow at 3.09
times its debt, free cash flow at 1.66 times its debt. Tourmaline is
close behind at 0.40 times and 2.09 times. Imperial has the lowest
leverage of the energy names at 0.50 times and the highest operating
cash flow cover in energy at 1.44 times. Barrick is joint top of the
scorecard at 17 and carries only 0.17 times net leverage. Suncor is
the quietest name in the group: 14 on the scorecard, 0.93 times
leverage, 12.8 times coverage, operating cash flow above its debt.

### Can pay: CNQ, Cenovus, Cameco

CNQ and Cenovus sit at the bottom of the scorecard, 9 and 12, and both
can pay. Their scores are disclosure gaps. Bill C-59 stripped the
environmental content from their 2024 reports, so CNQ scored 0 on
climate targets and 0 on disclosure, and Cenovus scored 0 on climate
targets. Their balance sheets are strong: CNQ at 1.22 times leverage
and 13.0 times coverage, Cenovus at 1.11 times and 7.3 times. A reader
who only saw the scorecard would call CNQ the worst company in the
set. A reader who only saw the credit models would call it one of the
strongest. Both readings are in the numbers; the disclosure note is
what reconciles them.

Cameco is the moderate case. It scores 12, held back by the worst
safety figure in the set at 2.26 injuries per 200,000 hours, 25% above
its own target. Its credit is middle of the set: 1.93 times leverage,
3.0 times coverage, operating cash flow at 0.71 times its debt. It can
pay, but its cash flow depends on the uranium price, and its equity
volatility in 2024 was the highest in the set at 42.9% a year.

### Constrained: Nutrien and Methanex

Both scored 15 out of 20, joint fourth on the scorecard, and both are
the most leveraged names in the set. Methanex is at 4.05 times
EBITDA, 2.93 times net, with the highest Zmijewski probability at
36.3% and the thinnest cash flow cover: operating cash flow at 0.23
times its debt, free cash flow at 0.17 times. Nutrien is at 3.04
times, 2.84 times net, Zmijewski 20.0%, operating cash flow at 0.28
times its debt and free cash flow at 0.11 times. Methanex leverage is
persistent, not one bad year: 3.79 times in 2023 and 4.04 times in
2025. Their transition spending, low-carbon methanol for Methanex and
low-carbon ammonia and potash efficiency for Nutrien, has to be funded
from operations that are already levered, and it competes directly
with deleveraging. A high sustainability score does not create room
to spend.

### Special cases: Teck and West Fraser

Teck is joint top of the scorecard at 17 and the most complicated
credit in the set. Gross leverage of 5.59 times is the highest in the
set, and reported coverage of 0.07 times is the lowest. Net leverage
is 1.33 times, because the coal sale left $7,587 million of cash on
the balance sheet at the end of 2024. On adjusted EBIT, coverage is
1.43 times, Z-prime 3.79 and Springate 0.50, still below the 0.862
Springate cut-off. Even adjusted, Teck is weak on earnings cover,
because interest expense of $775 million is large next to post-coal
earnings, and 2024 free cash flow was only $155 million. Merton gives
a distance to default of 7.14, far from default. The honest reading:
Teck is not in distress, but its ability to fund transition
investment depends on copper earnings and the Quebrada Blanca ramp,
not on the cash pile alone, and the cash will not last if it is spent
without earnings behind it.

West Fraser is the opposite disagreement. Z-prime calls it the safest
name in the set at 6.53, because it is net cash: $920 million of cash
against $329 million of debt, net debt to EBITDA of -0.67 times.
Springate calls it distressed at 0.46, because 2024 EBIT was only
$92 million after the lumber downturn. A net-cash company is not in
distress. Its earnings collapsed for a year; its balance sheet did
not. The constraint on West Fraser is earnings cyclicality, not debt.

## What each part alone would miss

The scorecard alone would rank CNQ last and Nutrien and Methanex
joint fourth. The credit models reverse both calls. The credit models
alone would call Teck the weakest name and West Fraser distressed.
The scorecard and the cash positions reverse both calls. Part A alone
would say the transition is stalled in production, because the fossil
share of what Canada produces rose to 88.5%. The company-level answer
is that the cash to fund the transition exists in the producers'
balance sheets. The energy names that can pay comfortably, CNQ,
Suncor, Imperial, Cenovus and Tourmaline, earned their cash from the
fuels the transition is meant to replace, and their combined operating
cash flow in 2024 was about $47 billion. Whether they spend it on
transition projects is a choice, not a constraint.

## Limits

One year, 2024, for both parts. The scores are disclosure-based: a
company that reports more scores higher, and Bill C-59 made the
energy scores partly a measure of what was published, not what was
done. The credit models are backward-looking and use reported
earnings, so one-off items like Teck's write-off move them. Merton
says no company is near default, but that reflects market values at
31 December 2024. Ability to pay is not willingness to spend: this
note does not test capital allocation plans, and a company with room
on its balance sheet may still choose dividends and buybacks over
transition investment. That choice is the next question, and it needs
the companies' capital plans, not their 2024 financials.
